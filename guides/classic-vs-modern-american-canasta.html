Classic Canasta vs Modern American Canasta: What Is Different

By Will McCullough, DigiRune Studios · November 19, 2026

Short answer: they share a name, two decks with jokers, and seven-card canastas, but they are different games. Classic Canasta deals 11 cards, needs one canasta to go out and plays to 5,000. Modern American Canasta deals 13 cards, needs two canastas to go out, has special rules for sevens and aces, asks for a much bigger first meld, and plays to 8,500. If half your table learned one and half learned the other, settle which game you are playing before the first deal.

Canasta is said to have started in Montevideo, Uruguay, in 1939, and in the 1950s it was extremely fashionable. In North America the game kept changing, and the modern version is now the more common one there. Classic Canasta is still widely played in Europe and elsewhere. That is how two players who both "know Canasta" can sit down and disagree on the very first hand.

Both versions use the same card values: jokers 50, twos and aces 20, eight through king 10, four through seven 5. Natural canastas score 500 and mixed canastas 300 in both (in Modern American, pure canastas of sevens or aces score 2,500 instead; a mixed canasta of aces is still 300, and sevens cannot be mixed).

What actually feels different at the table

The start is slower in Modern American. A 125-point first meld is a big hurdle next to classic's 50, and in Modern American it has to come from your own hand, with a pure meld of three natural cards (or a meld of wild cards) already in place. Expect more turns of drawing and discarding before anyone puts cards down.

Wild cards are scarcer. Classic players are used to dropping twos and jokers wherever they help. In Modern American a canasta can hold only two wild cards, and tables that play the newer "rule of five" add that, after your first meld, a meld has to grow to five real cards before a wild card can go on it (a meld made only of wild cards aside). That changes how you hold your hand.

Sevens and aces become a gamble. In classic they are ordinary cards. In Modern American a finished pure canasta of sevens or aces is worth 2,500, but start one and fail to finish it and the same 2,500 comes off your score. Holding three or more of them when the hand ends also costs you.

Threes work differently. Classic players remember red threes as a bonus and black threes as a blocking discard. In Modern American threes are normally laid out on the table as they arrive, red or black, and whether those threes help or hurt depends on how many canastas your team finishes.

Going out takes longer. Two canastas instead of one, plus a card to discard at the end, so hands run longer and totals run higher, and the game runs to 8,500.

Settle it before the first deal

Even inside each version, groups play their own table rules. The card game rules site pagat.com notes that, as far as its author knows, there is no single set of Modern American rules everyone accepts as correct, and advises finding out a group's table rules before you join. So when a table mixes players from both camps, take two minutes:

  • Say out loud which version you are playing, classic or Modern American, and what score ends the game.
  • Agree on the first meld minimums and how the discard pile can be taken. Those two rules often cause arguments.
  • If you play Modern American, confirm the sevens and aces penalties and how threes are scored.
  • Write the answers at the top of the score sheet so nobody has to remember them in round three.
